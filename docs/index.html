The .ada Format

A text you can question

Perig Gouanvic · Montréal · working draft, November 2026
See an example GitHub
“[Written words:] you might think they spoke as if they had intelligence, but if you question them, wishing to know about their sayings, they always say only one and the same thing.”
Socrates, in Plato, Phaedrus 275d, trans. H. N. Fowler (1914), Perseus

An .ada file is a text that travels with its author's guidance: what it claims, what a reader needs to know first, where it is usually misread, which objections the author has already met, and what must never be changed.

You open it with the AI you already use. Instead of reading it once, front to back, you question it. The answers stay inside the frame the author set, and when a question falls outside it, the author can find out.

Why this is needed

Anyone who writes keeps asking: who am I talking to? And keeps thinking: I should add this, for them. There is no ideal reader, because no two readers are alike. The runner wants a story while running, the student wants the physiology, the skeptic wants the sources.

So the author faces the same choice every time. Simplify, and lose precision. Keep it exact, and lose most of the readers who needed it. Write several versions, and pay for each one, then watch them drift apart.

Readers already work around this by pasting texts into an AI and asking for a summary. That helps, but the summary is the AI's reading, not the author's. It cannot know which shortcut is a betrayal and which one is fine. An .ada file gives it that knowledge.

One example

An .ada file is a small package of readable text files: the text itself (as the author wants the AI to know it), a manifest, instructions for the AI that will answer, and the questions the author has left open. Here is a simplified view of what it could contain for this very page. (Illustrative. The structure is still being stabilized.) 

ada: "0.1-draft"
title: "The .ada Format"
author: "Perig Gouanvic"
text: "prompt.md"             # the text, written for the AI that answers

intent: >
  Show writers of dense texts that they can stay precise
  and still be understood, by encoding their own guidance.

concepts:
  - id: trilemma
    claim: "Simplify, stay exact, or multiply versions: each choice loses something."
    prerequisites: []
    misreadings:
      - reading: ".ada simplifies texts"
        correction: "Nothing is cut once and for all. Adaptation happens in dialogue, within the author's limits."

objections:
  - question: "Isn't this just asking an AI for a summary?"
    answer: "A summary is the AI's reading. An .ada file carries the author's reading."

voice:
  must_keep:  ["the trilemma", "open legal questions stay open"]
  may_adapt:  [vocabulary, length, examples, language]
  never:      ["attribute to the author a position they have not taken"]

feedback:
  when_uncovered: "Offer to draft a short note to the author."

Two readers open the same file.

A tenants' committee organizer

I have five minutes. What does this change for me?

If someone sends your committee a 60-page housing report as an .ada file, you can ask it “what does this mean for our building?” and get an answer the report's author has prepared for, in plain words, without the author writing a separate version for you.

A philosopher

Isn't this a prompt with a new name?

The author expected that objection: a prompt tells the AI what to do; this file tells it what the author meant and what must not be lost. Whether that difference holds up in practice is not something the file settles. Do you want me to draft that question for the author?

How it works

1

The author is interviewed

The author gives a text to an AI that acts as an interviewer, playing the readers the author did not write for. “A runner wants something to listen to on the road: what would you tell them?” “A physiology student asks why this muscle tires first: is it in the text?” The answers, and the material they reveal is missing, become the .ada file.

2

The reader asks

The reader opens the file with their own AI and questions it: “explain this simply”, “give me the technical version”, “how does this apply to my situation?”, “challenge this argument”. The AI speaks for the text, within the author's limits.

3

The text learns

When a question is not covered, the AI offers to draft a short note to the author, with the context already written. The author answers once, adds it to the file, and every later reader benefits.

Why a file extension, then? Not as a barrier. An .ada file is a zip archive of plain text files (Markdown and JSON) that a person can open and current AIs read natively. The extension is a signal: this text is meant to be questioned, and it carries instructions for whoever answers.

What it does not promise

  • Fidelity is bounded, not guaranteed. The file narrows what an AI can say in the author's name. It does not make the AI infallible.
  • It needs an AI. A reader without access is left with the plain text. Public access (libraries, community services) is part of the question, not an afterthought.
  • Some difficulty is worth keeping. An author can mark passages where the effort of reading is the point, and ask the AI not to smooth them away.
  • It is early. There is one real .ada file so far (JP³), no stable schema and no tested encoder. See where things stand.

Where things stand

This page is a manifesto, revised in November 2026. The next steps, in order:

  1. Stabilize the draft specification v0.1 (see appendix A), based on the first .ada file.
  2. An interviewing prompt for authors, and a reading prompt for readers' AIs (needed until AIs recognize the format on their own).
  3. A first real case: JP³, a long and dense text, already packaged as jp3.ada, to be tested with readers of very different backgrounds.
  4. This page itself, published as manifesto.ada.
  5. What readers ask, and what the file failed to cover, used to revise the schema.

Help is welcome from developers (schema, tools), accessibility researchers (testing with real readers), philosophers, writers with a text that deserves more readers, and legal scholars (see open questions). Open an issue on GitHub.

An old question

Socrates

Written words cannot answer when questioned. Irony: we know this complaint only through Plato's writing.

Diderot and d'Alembert

The Encyclopédie and its cross-references let readers build their own path through knowledge, and aimed to reach readers the Latin libraries excluded.

Ted Nelson

Hypertext promised documents in living connection with their sources. Nelson judged the Web that followed to be “paper under glass”.

Ada Lovelace

She saw that a machine could manipulate symbols, not only numbers. The format carries her name: a document that is partly a program for its own conversation.

A. Draft structure of an .ada file

The first real .ada file (JP³, September 2026) is a zip archive containing manifest.json, ada-usage.md, prompt.md and open_questions.md. The list below describes what these files are meant to carry. The draft specification gives the details: spec/ada-v0.1.md, manifest schema, and a validator.

  • Text: the content as the author wants the AI to know it. In JP³ it is a version rewritten for the AI; whether the original should also travel unchanged is an open question.
  • Intent: what the author wants the reader to come away with.
  • Concepts: key claims, their dependencies (what must be understood first), known misreadings.
  • Entry points: a short summary, a central question, a concrete case, an analogy.
  • Objections: questions the author has already met, with the author's answers.
  • Epistemic notes: how certain each claim is, sources, open questions.
  • Voice: what must be kept, what may be adapted (vocabulary, length, examples, language), what must never be said in the author's name.
  • Accessibility hints: likely difficult passages, useful alternative forms (diagram, timeline, audio).
  • Feedback: how and when to propose a note to the author.
B. How it compares with existing approaches
  • Plain language, Easy Read (FALC): one simplified version, written by hand. .ada keeps the full content and lets each reader move between simple and full detail.
  • Universal Design for Learning (UDL): a framework for anticipating learner diversity in teaching. .ada applies the same idea to any document, written by anyone.
  • Prompt templates: reusable instructions for developers. .ada is written by an author for readers, about one specific text.
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP): infrastructure connecting AI applications to tools and data. .ada is not infrastructure; it is a document between two people, with an AI in the middle.
  • Shared AI outputs (artifacts, generated summaries): a fixed result. .ada shares what is needed to produce many answers, not one.

What seems new is the combination: human to human, encoded by the author from the start, and open to questioning rather than delivered in one adapted version.

C. How different readers might approach this page
  • The skeptical technologist asks “show me it is not vaporware”, and is pointed to the example, the draft structure and the status.
  • The accessibility advocate asks who is left out, and is pointed to the limits and to the question of public access.
  • The philosopher asks about lineage and stakes, and is pointed to the history and to appendix E.
  • The busy creator asks “can I use this now?”, and gets an honest answer: not yet, here is what comes first.
  • The reader who does not get it is offered a concrete case first: “imagine a report that answers your questions about it, the way its author would.”
D. Open questions: other people's texts, rights, risks

Encoding texts one did not write. One could encode an existing hermetic paper, a law or a classic, with the help of external sources, and redistribute it. This raises questions nobody has settled:

  • Is it closer to translation, to commentary, or to a derivative work?
  • Can it fall under accessibility exceptions that exist in many jurisdictions?
  • Who speaks for the text when the encoder is not the author?

Risks worth naming.

  • Unequal access: if only some readers have an AI, the gap widens.
  • Lost effort: hard reading builds abilities; readers should be able to ask for the demanding version.
  • Control: who decides what the AI may say? The file should be open and readable by anyone.
  • Comfort: if everyone gets the version that suits them, nobody meets a way of thinking unlike their own.
E. The author's position: difficulty and gatekeeping

This appendix states a personal and contested position. It is not part of the format.

Some texts are difficult because their subject is. Others are difficult in a way that also works as a gate: a style that sorts readers before they reach the argument. I think parts of Heidegger, Derrida and Lacan have been used this way, at least by some of their institutions of commentary, even if their admirers see a necessary density there.

An .ada reading of such texts could do two things at once: keep the full text for those who want its density, and let a newcomer ask “what exactly is being claimed here?” If the answer turns out to be rich, the difficulty was earned. If it turns out to be thin, that is worth knowing too. Separating real difficulty from exclusion is, to me, one of the most interesting uses of the format.

Philosophical notes. The interviewing step is a kind of maieutics turned toward the author: questions that help them say what they meant. The idea of a document carrying the rules of its own variations recalls Maturana and Varela's autopoiesis, loosely. And the demand that cognitive diversity count as a matter of justice, not of accommodation, comes from work on neurodiversity and epistemic justice.
